# 1. Reward neuroscience and kids/teen psychology

Deep study. Evidence grades: **[S]** strong, **[M]** moderate, **[P]** practitioner, **[A]** our analysis.

---

## 1.1 Who plays Roblox (Q1 2026, age-checked users)
- **Age split:** under 13 is **36%**, 13–17 is **38%**, 18+ is **26%**. Roblox has 132M DAU (+35% YoY), 31B hours engaged (+43%), and 31M monthly unique payers (+52%). 51% of DAU worldwide and 65% in the US are age-checked (Roblox Q1 2026 shareholder letter). [S]
- **Implication:** about three quarters of players are minors. Design every reward system for a 9–15-year-old brain first. [A]

## 1.2 How the reward system works
| Finding | Evidence | Design implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Dopamine encodes reward prediction error.** Neurons fire for better-than-expected outcomes, stay flat for fully predicted ones, and dip when an expected reward is omitted. | Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997, *Science* [S] | Surprise drives the response. Fixed rewards stop motivating over time, and an expected reward that doesn't arrive feels like a loss. |
| **Wanting and liking are separate.** Dopamine drives *wanting* (pursuit). *Liking* (pleasure) is a separate, opioid-linked system. | Berridge & Robinson 1998 [S] | Compulsion is wanting without liking, where players say "I keep playing but it's not fun." Those players churn and don't recommend the game. Aim for wanting and liking that point the same way. |
| **Games release dopamine.** A PET scan showed striatal dopamine release during video game play. | Koepp et al. 1998, *Nature* [S] | The mechanism is real. Use it for genuine fun. |
| **Variable schedules are learned faster and resist extinction longer.** | Ferster & Skinner 1957 [S] | Use variable rewards in free play. Paid variable rewards are gambling-like ([02](02-gambling-monetisation.md)). |
| **Loss aversion.** Losses weigh about 2x equivalent gains. | Kahneman & Tversky 1979 [S] | Theft, death and expiry hit hard. They're powerful for tension, but also cause rage-quits and distress in kids. |
| **Arousal.** Moderate arousal improves focus, while too much fatigues (Yerkes–Dodson). | [S, classic] | A chase followed by escape works best as spike then release. Constant panic burns players out. |

## 1.3 Motivation: why players come back
- **Self-determination theory (PENS).** Satisfying **competence, autonomy and relatedness** predicted enjoyment, wanting to continue, and future play across several games, more strongly than genre or graphics (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006; Przybylski et al. 2010). [S]
- **Overjustification.** A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found tangible rewards reduce intrinsic interest, especially when they feel controlling. Rewards framed as feedback ("you got better") do less harm (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999). The core activity must be fun *before* rewards are added. [S]
- **Curiosity** is the strongest driver of moment-to-moment juice motivation and free-choice playtime (Kao et al. 2024, see [03](03-reward-presentation.md)). [S-]
- **Flow** comes from clear goals, immediate feedback and challenge just above skill (Csikszentmihalyi 1990). Chen (2007) argues players should choose their own difficulty rather than follow a fixed curve. [S/P]
- **Player motives differ.** Yee (2006) found achievement, social and immersion motives, and Bartle (1996) proposed achievers, explorers, socialisers and killers. On Roblox, add **creators** (building, decorating). Hits usually serve two or more of these groups. [M/P]

## 1.4 The adolescent brain (13–17, 38% of players)
- **Dual-systems model.** Reward sensitivity (ventral striatum) peaks in mid-adolescence, while cognitive control (prefrontal cortex) matures into the mid-20s (Steinberg 2008; Casey et al. 2008). Teens are more novelty- and reward-seeking. [S]
- **Peers amplify it.** Teens took more risks when peers watched, but adults didn't, and the effect showed up in reward-region activity (Chein et al. 2011, *Dev Science*). [S]
- **Social reward runs both ways.** In early adolescents (n=136, ages 11–14), ventral striatum sensitivity to social reward and punishment increased risk-taking when peers were seen as deviant and *reduced* it when peers were prosocial (Telzer et al. 2020, *Child Development*). A game's norms steer behaviour. [M]
- **Likes and status.** Teens' reward circuitry responds strongly to peer approval, such as "likes" on their own photos (Sherman et al. 2016, *Psych Science*). [M]
- **Implications:**
  - Public status (auras, titles, rare items) is especially motivating for teens.
  - Risk choices made in front of peers are compelling, e.g. Huss Valley's Hero vs Chicken choice ([09](09-virality-saturation.md)).
  - Prosocial norms such as revives, gifting and teamwork can be rewarded and will spread. [A]

## 1.5 Children (under 13, 36% of players)
- **Concrete thinking** (Piaget's concrete-operational stage, about ages 7–11). Kids handle tangible, visible goals well and abstract systems poorly. Show, don't explain. [S, classic]
- **Small working memory and limited reading.** Use icons, arrows and visual tutorials. Many players are young or playing in a second language (Hodent 2017). [S/P]
- **Weak persuasion knowledge.** Children are less able to recognise and resist persuasive intent such as advertising and urgency (John 1999; Sydney 2026, see [02](02-gambling-monetisation.md)). Pressure tactics on this audience are both unethical and a regulatory target. [S]
- **Strong appeals:** collecting, nurturing pets (Adopt Me!), dressing up, creating, absurd humour (brainrot), and bright, saturated visuals. [P]
- **Strong reactions to unfairness and loss.** Viral videos of children crying over stolen brainrots spread the game but also caused reputational and wellbeing harm. [M]
- **Session health.** The WHO lists gaming disorder in ICD-11. Very long single sessions by young players are a warning sign, not a success metric. Build natural stopping points, such as a completed run or a day/night cycle. [S]

## 1.6 Ethics: the healthy-engagement test
A mechanic is healthy if it (a) is fun without its timers, (b) makes the player feel capable, free and connected, and (c) doesn't punish leaving. Mechanics that rely on wanting without liking, pressure, or fear of loss produce short spikes followed by churn, refunds, and parents banning the game (*Sticky by Design*). [A]

## 1.7 Audit criteria
- [ ] Is there an outcome the player didn't fully predict within the first 60 seconds?
- [ ] Are rewards varied in size and timing, rather than all flat and fixed?
- [ ] Is the core action fun with rewards removed (overjustification test)?
- [ ] Competence: is skill growth visible? Autonomy: are there two or more meaningful choices, such as goals, routes or builds? Relatedness: is there a reason to play with others?
- [ ] Is there something to be curious about (hidden areas, unknown outcomes, secrets)?
- [ ] Is difficulty self-selectable, or does it ease after repeated failure?
- [ ] Do at least two player motives (achiever, explorer, socialiser, competitor, creator) get served?
- [ ] Teens: is there peer-visible status, and are there peer-visible risk choices?
- [ ] Are prosocial behaviours (revive, gift, help) rewarded?
- [ ] Under-13s: can the game be understood with no reading, and are its goals concrete and visible?
- [ ] Do loss mechanics sting without causing distress (recoverable, protected, never total)?
- [ ] Are there natural stopping points, with nothing that punishes leaving?
